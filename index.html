What is corporate governance?

Click each party to see what can go wrong

Definition
The system of rules, practices and processes by which a company is directed and controlled — and the way it balances the interests of everyone with a stake in it.

The core problem
Ownership and control sit with different people. The people who own the company are not the people running it — so their interests can drift apart. Governance is simply the machinery built to keep them aligned. This is what agency theory (Jensen & Meckling, 1976) studies.

The three pillars: E, S and G

Click a pillar

Why G comes first
Governance is the only pillar you can verify from public documents — a board's composition and an auditor's report are facts on record. E and S are largely what the company chooses to tell you. So a company with weak governance cannot be trusted on its own E and S reporting either. G is the gatekeeper.

Which is most used?
Integration. Exclusion is the easiest to explain but the bluntest — you lose whole industries from your portfolio. Integration is now the mainstream approach because it does not require a separate process: the analyst simply builds the risk into the valuation they were already doing.
Challenges
07 / 10

Why this is harder than it sounds

1
The ratings do not agree

Different agencies rate the same company very differently, because each one measures different things and weights them differently. There is no single agreed scorecard.

2
Greenwashing

Most of this information is reported by the company about itself. A firm can look responsible on paper simply by writing a longer sustainability report.

3
It rewards disclosure, not performance

Scores often go up when a company reports more — not when it actually behaves better.

4
Short-term pressure

Governance improvements pay off over five to ten years, but fund managers are judged every three months.

How much do the raters agree?
Average agreement between six major ESG rating agencies, compared with credit ratings. 1.00 would mean perfect agreement.
ESG ratings0.54
Credit ratings0.99
Credit rating agencies almost always agree. ESG agencies agree only about half the time — so two funds using the same word can hold completely different companies.
None of this makes ESG useless. It means the ratings are a starting point, not an answer — you still have to read the company's own filings.

Recommendations & conclusion
10 / 10

What we would recommend

For investors
  • Treat governance as a pass/fail test, and E and S as factors that adjust the valuation.
  • Build it into the normal valuation rather than keeping a separate ESG score on the side.
  • Use ratings as a starting point, never a conclusion — read the company's own filings.
  • Judge engagement on what actually changed, not how many letters were written.
For companies
  • Genuine board independence matters more than meeting the minimum count.
  • Disclose dealings with the owner's other businesses before someone else finds them.
  • Treat CSR as a long-term investment in trust, not a tax to be paid and forgotten.
  • Report outcomes, not just amounts spent.
For regulators
  • Move towards one common reporting standard so companies can be compared.
  • Require independent checking of sustainability data, the way accounts are audited.
  • Regulate the rating agencies, not only the companies they rate.
  • Strengthen the voting rights of small shareholders.
In conclusion: corporate governance and social responsibility are no longer a filter applied after an investment decision. Done properly they are part of how the decision is made — a judgement about risk and the return investors will demand, not a statement of values.
